# Low-rank approximation

A data matrix is almost never exactly rank-deficient, and almost never genuinely full-rank in a way
that matters. Its singular values decrease, usually fast, and the practical question is not "what
is the rank" but **"how many components do I need to keep?"** This lesson answers that question in
the only way that has a theorem behind it: keep the `k` largest singular values, and know exactly
what the resulting error is.

The source for this lesson is MML section 4.6 (printed pp.129-134), where the SVD is used to build
approximations from rank-1 outer products, together with section 10.4 (printed pp.333-335), which
connects low-rank approximation to eigenvector computation in PCA.

**One thing to know before reading.** The supplied text states the optimality result for the
**spectral norm** (its Theorem 4.25, equations 4.94 and 4.95). It never states the Frobenius-norm
version, and the word "Frobenius" does not appear anywhere in the supplied markdown — which this
platform checked rather than assumed. Since this competency's own outcome asks for the
Frobenius-norm result, the lesson marks clearly which parts are sourced and which are not. The
coverage report records the gap; the honesty is part of the content, not a footnote to it.

## Part 1 — The truncation, and the two theorems about it

### 1.1 The object

From `LSN-MATH-07-01`, every matrix `A ∈ R^{m×n}` of rank `r` can be written as a sum of `r` rank-1
matrices:

$$
A = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top}, \qquad \sigma_1 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r > 0
$$

(MML equation 4.90 states exactly this form; the source uses it to build approximations of an
image, its Figure 4.11). Define the **rank-k truncation**

$$
\hat{A}(k) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top} \qquad (k \le r).
$$

`Â(k)` is the matrix you get by keeping the `k` largest terms and deleting the rest. It has rank at
most `k` by construction, and it costs `k(m + n + 1)` numbers to store rather than `mn` — which is
the whole practical point.

### 1.2 What the truncation achieves (proved here)

The residual is exactly the tail of the sum:

$$
A - \hat{A}(k) = \sum_{i=k+1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top},
$$

which is itself a valid SVD, with singular values `σ_{k+1}, ..., σ_r`. Therefore, using
`‖M‖₂ = σ₁(M)` (Theorem 4.24) and `‖M‖_F² = Σσᵢ(M)²` (`LSN-MATH-07-01`, exercise 4):

$$
\|A - \hat{A}(k)\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1},
\qquad
\|A - \hat{A}(k)\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=k+1}^{r} \sigma_i^2 .
$$

These two identities are the reason the singular values are the right summary of a matrix. The
first says the worst-case directional error is the next singular value; the second gives the total
squared error in a form you can turn into a percentage.

### 1.3 What the source states (Theorem 4.25, Eckart-Young)

MML's Theorem 4.25 states that for `Â(k)` as above,

$$
\hat{A}(k) = \operatorname*{argmin}_{\operatorname{rk}(B) = k} \|A - B\|_2,
\qquad
\|A - \hat{A}(k)\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}
$$

(its equations 4.94 and 4.95). The source also explains why the second equation holds, by observing
that the residual is the tail sum and that the largest singular value of a matrix is its spectral
norm. It states the optimality — that no rank-`k` matrix does better — and cites Eckart and Young
(1936).

**What is sourced and what is not.** The achievement (`σ_{k+1}` is attained) is in the source. The
optimality claim is stated in the source. The **proof** of optimality, for the spectral norm, is
written out in Part 2 below: the source leaves it to the citation, and reconstructing it is what
makes the theorem usable. The **Frobenius** version of the optimality statement is not in the
source at all, and the lesson marks it as such.

## Part 2 — Why no rank-k matrix can do better (spectral norm)

This is the proof the source does not give. It is short, and every step is a fact from earlier in
the curriculum.

**Claim.** For every `B` with `rk(B) ≤ k`, `‖A − B‖₂ ≥ σ_{k+1}`.

**Proof.** The idea is a dimension count. A rank-`k` matrix cannot annihilate a
`(k+1)`-dimensional subspace, so somewhere in the span of the first `k+1` right-singular vectors
it must fail to cancel `A`, and there `A` is at least as large as `σ_{k+1}`.

1. **The null space is big.** `Bx = 0` has a solution space of dimension
   `dim ker B ≥ n − rk(B) ≥ n − k` (rank-nullity).
2. **The other space is small but non-trivial.** Let
   `V_{k+1} = span{v₁, ..., v_{k+1}}`, of dimension `k+1` (the right-singular vectors are
   orthonormal, hence independent).
3. **They must intersect.** Two subspaces of `R^n` with dimensions `d₁` and `d₂` always intersect
   in dimension at least `d₁ + d₂ − n`. Here that is `(n − k) + (k + 1) − n = 1`, so there is a
   non-zero vector `x` with `Bx = 0` and `x ∈ V_{k+1}`. Choose `‖x‖ = 1`.
4. **`A` is large on that vector.** Write `x = Σ_{i≤k+1} cᵢvᵢ` with `Σcᵢ² = 1`. Then
   `Ax = Σ_{i≤k+1} cᵢσᵢuᵢ` and, because the `uᵢ` are orthonormal,
   `‖Ax‖² = Σ_{i≤k+1} cᵢ²σᵢ² ≥ σ_{k+1}² Σ_{i≤k+1} cᵢ² = σ_{k+1}²`.
5. **Conclusion.** `‖A − B‖₂ = max_{‖y‖=1}‖(A − B)y‖ ≥ ‖(A − B)x‖ = ‖Ax‖ ≥ σ_{k+1}`. ∎

The same argument, applied to every `i ≤ k`, gives `σ_{k+i}(A) ≤ σ_i(A − B)` — the interlacing
inequality for singular values. For the Frobenius norm it can be sharpened into a full proof that
`Â(k)` is optimal there too, but that argument uses the von Neumann trace inequality, which is not
in the supplied source and is not reconstructed here. What follows from Part 1.2 is the weaker but
still useful statement that `Â(k)` **attains** `Σ_{i>k}σᵢ²`, and the literature result is that this
is the best any rank-`k` matrix can do. The competency records this as the outstanding gap.

## Part 3 — Two ways to measure the error, and when to use which

`σ_{k+1}` and `Σ_{i>k}σᵢ²` answer different questions, and choosing the wrong one produces a
misleading answer.

### 3.1 Spectral error: the worst direction

`‖A − Â(k)‖₂ = σ_{k+1}`. Use it when the downstream use is sensitive to a *single* direction: it is
the maximum stretch by which any unit vector can still be distorted. If you discard a component
with `σ_{k+1} = 100` while all others are below `1`, the spectral error is `100` — large — even
though that component might carry a negligible fraction of the total energy. Spectral error is
therefore the right measure for questions of the form "could one direction hurt me?" and the wrong
one for questions of the form "how much of the data did I keep on average?".

### 3.2 Frobenius error: total squared error

`‖A − Â(k)‖_F² = Σ_{i>k}σᵢ²`. This is the average-case measure: it is the sum of squared entrywise
errors, so it is the natural one when the matrix is a batch of observations and you care about the
total distortion. Normalising gives two numbers that are in constant use:

$$
\text{relative error} = \frac{\|A - \hat{A}(k)\|_F}{\|A\|_F}
= \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}},
\qquad
\text{retained energy} = 1 - \left(\frac{\|A - \hat{A}(k)\|_F}{\|A\|_F}\right)^2 .
$$

The square root is worth pausing over, because it is where percentage claims about compression go
wrong. "Retains 99% of the energy" means a relative error of about 10%, not 1%. The two quantities
differ by a square, and both are routinely quoted as "the error".

### 3.3 Rank, numerical rank, and the decision that hides in the word

Mathematically the rank is the number of nonzero singular values. Computationally, computed
singular values are almost never exactly zero, so the rank is what you decide it is: the number of
singular values above a threshold. That threshold is a decision about how much error you are
willing to accept, and its consequences are measurable (exercise 5). `numpy.linalg.matrix_rank`
uses `max(m, n) · ε · σ₁` by default, which is the round-off level; a data analyst discards far
more than that, because the small singular values of a data matrix are as likely to be noise as
signal. Choosing `k` from data rather than from round-off is a modelling decision — and one the
supplied source does not cover.

## Part 4 — The trade-off, measured

The demonstration below uses a 24x24 matrix built by `tools/build_demo_data.py` from three explicit
formulas: a smooth Gaussian bump, a diagonal ridge, and a fine high-frequency texture. The three
decay at different rates, so the reconstruction lets you see *what* survives truncation, not merely
that something does. It is a teaching object built for this lesson, not a dataset, and the builder
script is the record of how it was made.

Its singular values, computed once and stored in `content/demos/rank-truncation.json`, begin

```
6.453, 2.038, 1.352, 0.901, 0.887, 0.873, 0.616, 0.336, 0.330, 0.161, ... 0.000
```

and the recorded trade-off is:

| k | stored entries (of 576) | relative Frobenius error | energy retained | spectral error `σ_{k+1}` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 49 (9%) | 0.421 | 82.2% | 2.038 |
| 3 | 147 (26%) | 0.244 | 94.1% | 0.901 |
| 5 | 245 (43%) | 0.167 | 97.2% | 0.873 |
| 8 | 392 (68%) | 0.056 | 99.7% | 0.330 |
| 12 | 588 (102%) | 0.005 | 99.998% | 0.030 |

Two things in that table are the lesson in miniature. First, **one component captures 82% of the
energy**: the smooth bump is nearly rank-1, and that is what a fast-decaying spectrum means.
Second, **the trade-off has a hard edge**: at `k = 12` the factorisation needs more numbers than the
matrix itself (588 > 576), so the representation is no longer a compression. The exact break-even
point is derived in exercise 4.

The spectral column is not an independent measurement: it is `σ_{k+1}` read off the spectrum, which
is precisely the content of the theorem. The interactive version lets you move `k` and watch both
the error figures and the reconstruction change together.

## Part 5 — Applying it to a data matrix

Rank truncation on a data matrix has an interpretation that the arithmetic alone does not give,
and it is the one that matters in practice.

**The setup.** Let `X ∈ R^{n×d}` hold `n` observations of `d` features, centred so each column has
mean zero. The truncated SVD of `X` is

$$
X \approx \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top},
$$

where $v_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a direction in feature space, $u_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ records how much each
observation lies along it, and $\sigma_i$ measures how much of the data's variation that direction accounts for.

**What the pieces mean.**

- **$v_1$ is the direction of greatest variance in the data** — the first principal component.
  Section 10.4 of the source makes this connection explicit, showing that PCA's projection
  directions are obtained from the eigendecomposition (or the SVD) of the centred data, and that
  the low-rank approximation and the variance-maximising projection are two readings of the same
  decomposition. A separate lesson (`ML-07`) takes up PCA itself, including the standardisation
  decision that this paragraph glosses over: whether to centre, and whether to scale each feature,
  changes `v₁` and is a modelling choice, not a mathematical consequence.
- **The $\sigma_i$ rank the directions by importance**, which is why "keep the top $k$" is not arbitrary:
  it is the choice that minimises squared reconstruction error among all rank-`k` approximations
  (Part 2).
- **The retained components are a low-dimensional embedding** of the observations: $u_i\sigma_i$ gives the
  coordinate of each observation along direction `i`. Only the first `k` are kept, so the data is
  represented in `k` dimensions. This is used for visualisation, for denoising, and as a
  preprocessing step before a linear model.

**What truncation is not.** It is not feature selection: the retained components are mixtures of
all the original features, so a linear model built on them is interpretable only through the
$v_i$. It is not noise removal in any guaranteed sense: a small singular value may be a weak but
real effect, and discarding it discards that effect. And it is not free: the truncation is chosen
by looking at the data, so downstream uncertainty statements that ignore this step are optimistic.

## Part 6 — Doing this with a computer

```python
import json, numpy as np

data = json.load(open("content/demos/rank-truncation.json"))
A = np.array(data["matrix"])
U = np.array(data["u"])
s = np.array(data["singular_values"])
Vt = np.array(data["vt"])

def truncate(k):
    return (U[:, :k] * s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]

for k in (1, 3, 8, 12):
    residual = np.linalg.norm(A - truncate(k), "fro")
    energy = np.sum(s[:k] ** 2) / np.sum(s ** 2)
    # The theorem's prediction, as a check on the arithmetic rather than a fresh result.
    predicted = np.sqrt(np.sum(s[k:] ** 2) / np.sum(s ** 2))
    assert abs(residual / np.linalg.norm(A) - predicted) < 1e-12
    print(f"k={k:2d}  relative error {residual / np.linalg.norm(A):.4f}  energy {energy:.6f}")
```

Three habits for this kind of work:

1. **Compute the error, do not estimate it.** `np.linalg.norm(A - truncate(k), "fro")` costs one
   matrix subtraction and is the ground truth; the closed form is a check on it.
2. **Watch the storage, not just the error.** A truncation that keeps 99.9% of the energy while
   storing more numbers than the original is not compression. The break-even rule is in exercise 4
   and applies to every "we compressed the data with SVD" claim you will read.
3. **Never let the truncation leak across a train/test split.** The components `vᵢ` are estimated
   from the data they are applied to; computing them on the full dataset before splitting means the
   test set influenced the features, which inflates every downstream metric. The mathematics is
   silent about this because it says nothing about where the matrix came from.

## Common misconceptions, with counterexamples

**1. "Keeping 99% of the energy gives 1% error."** It gives about 10% relative Frobenius error,
because energy is a squared quantity. Section 3.2 is the arithmetic.

**2. "The worst-case error is the Frobenius error."** No: `σ_{k+1} ≤ ‖A − Â(k)‖_F`, and the gap can
be enormous. The demo matrix at `k = 3` has a spectral error of `0.901` and a relative Frobenius
error of `0.244`; on a matrix whose tail has many small singular values the two differ by orders of
magnitude in the other direction.

**3. "The rank-k approximation is the same for every k in the sense that the components are
nested."** True for the SVD truncation — the first `k` components are reused for every larger `k` —
but not true for other low-rank fitting methods, and not true if you re-centre or rescale the data
between choices. Nestedness is a property of this construction, not of low-rank approximation
in general.

**4. "A small singular value means an unimportant feature."** It means a direction with little
variance. A low-variance direction can be perfectly predictive — this is a standard trap in
classification, where the discriminative direction may have small variance compared to nuisance
variation.

**5. "The truncation removes noise."** It removes variance below a threshold, which is not the same
thing. If the noise is large-variance, truncation keeps it and discards signal.

**6. "Rank truncation is lossless if the matrix is rank-deficient."** If the singular values below
`k` are exactly zero, then yes: `Â(k) = A` exactly, in exact arithmetic. In floating point the
"zero" singular values are around `1e-16 · σ₁`, so the reconstruction is accurate but not exact,
and the computed rank depends on the threshold (exercise 5).
